# Social Media Posts — written with the RTF formula

> Brief requirement (Stage 03): **3 social media posts written with the RTF formula**.
> RTF = **R**ole · **T**ask · **F**ormat. Each post below shows the exact prompt, then the final reviewed copy.
> Every number in these posts is sourced. See the [Content Review Log](../docs/04-content-review-log.md).

## Post 1 · LinkedIn
*Calendar day 1 (Mon) · Pillar 2: Deep Work Systems · Persona: Deniz & Selin*

**Prompt (RTF)**
```text
ROLE:   You are a calm, evidence-driven thought leader on the future of knowledge work,
        writing for the NEURALIS brand (see attached Brand Voice Guide).
TASK:   Write a LinkedIn post about the hidden cost of context switching for engineers and
        team leads. Make the reader rethink "quick questions" without blaming anyone.
        Use only this verified fact: Mark, González & Harris (2005) found interrupted work
        took ~25 minutes on average to resume.
FORMAT: One-line hook · 3 short paragraphs · 1 sourced statistic · end with an open question
        · 3 hashtags · max 1,300 characters · no emojis in the hook · no hype words.
```

**Final post**
> A "quick question" is never quick for the person answering it.
>
> In a field study of information workers, researchers at UC Irvine found that when work was interrupted, it took around **25 minutes** on average to get back to it, usually after two other tasks in between. *(Mark, González & Harris, CHI 2005)*
>
> That's not a productivity problem. It's a design problem. Most teams have never decided *when* interruptions are welcome, so the default is "always".
>
> The fix doesn't need a new tool. Start with one agreement: a protected focus block every morning, with questions batched for after.
>
> What's one rule your team uses to protect deep work?
>
> #DeepWork #FutureOfWork #EngineeringCulture

## Post 2 · Instagram carousel (5 slides)
*Calendar day 2 (Tue) · Pillar 1: Science of Focus · Persona: Deniz · Visual: [brainwave-synapses.jpg](../assets/images/brainwave-synapses.jpg)*

**Prompt (RTF)**
```text
ROLE:   You are NEURALIS's science editor. You turn neuroscience into simple, accurate visuals.
TASK:   Write slide copy and a caption for a 5-slide carousel explaining beta, alpha and theta
        brain states for knowledge workers. Be accurate: present the bands as associations,
        not switches. Do not promise that any product "puts you into" a state.
FORMAT: Slide 1 = hook (max 8 words) · Slides 2–4 = one band each (title + max 20 words)
        · Slide 5 = practical takeaway + soft CTA · Caption = 2 short lines + "save this" CTA
        + 3 hashtags.
```

**Final copy**

| Slide | Copy |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Your brain at work, in 3 states.** |
| 2 | **BETA · ~13–30 Hz.** Alert, effortful thinking. Great for a sprint, tiring as a permanent setting. |
| 3 | **ALPHA · ~8–12 Hz.** Relaxed wakefulness. Linked with filtering out what doesn't matter. |
| 4 | **THETA · ~4–7 Hz.** Drowsy, drifting, sometimes associative. Often shows up right before an idea. |
| 5 | **The takeaway:** focus isn't about forcing one state. It's about fewer interruptions, so your brain can settle. → *Link in bio* |

**Caption**
> Brain states blend. They aren't switches you can flip, but your environment can make settling easier. 🧠
> Save this for your next deep-work block.

## Post 3 · X / Twitter thread
*Calendar day 4 (Thu) · Pillar 3: Inside NEURALIS · Persona: Deniz (tech community)*

**Prompt (RTF)**
```text
ROLE:   You are a senior engineer and NEURALIS community lead who writes sharp, technical threads.
TASK:   Write a thread explaining why static focus playlists often stop helping after a while,
        and how adaptive audio is designed differently. Cite Garcia-Argibay et al. (2019) for
        auditory-beat evidence and say clearly that the evidence is promising but not settled.
        No competitor names. No medical claims.
FORMAT: 6 posts · each ≤ 280 characters · post 1 = counter-intuitive hook · last post = CTA
        · no hashtags · numbered "1/6".
```

**Final thread**
> **1/6** Your focus playlist probably works great for 20 minutes. Then you start skipping tracks. That's not a lack of discipline. It's how attention handles predictable input. 🧵
>
> **2/6** The brain tunes out repetitive, predictable sound. Useful for ignoring a fridge hum. Less useful when the sound was meant to keep you anchored.
>
> **3/6** Then there's lyrics. Language in music competes with language-heavy work like writing, reading docs or reviewing code. Non-lyrical sound competes less.
>
> **4/6** Rhythmic sound (binaural beats, amplitude-modulated music) is an active research area. A 2019 meta-analysis of 22 studies found a moderate effect on attention and memory, but results varied a lot with *how* it was used.
>
> **5/6** So instead of one "magic frequency", NEURALIS adapts: a gentler sound while you settle in, a sparser one once you're working, and subtle shifts when your typing pauses suggest you're drifting.
>
> **6/6** Promising science, honest claims, and sound that changes with your work. That's the bet. Beta access is open → neuralis.io *(fictional)*
